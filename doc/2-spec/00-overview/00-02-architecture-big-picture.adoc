== The platform on one page

.Strategy question
****
What strategy lets someone understand the whole platform before reading any of its forty-six other documents?
****

plantuml::diagrams/big-picture.puml[format=svg, align=center, pdfwidth=72%]

Everything below the load balancer runs in one Kubernetes cluster, in one region of Google Cloud; the message bus and the databases are services managed by the cloud provider.

.The platform in ten sentences
. An online store: visitors browse a catalog, customers fill a cart and buy, administrators manage products, stock and orders.
. Six services, each owning one part of the business and its own database: catalog, inventory, cart, order, payment, notification.
. Identity is not built: an identity provider holds accounts and roles, and every service checks the token it issues.
. The browser talks to one door, the web gateway, which keeps the session and forwards requests; no service is reachable from outside.
. Services talk to each other by messages on a bus, except where a fresh answer is needed at once: a price at checkout, the content of a cart.
. Placing an order is a *saga*: a sequence of steps across services, led by the order service, where each step that fails is undone by a compensating step.
. A service saves its data and the messages it must send in one database transaction, so that neither can exist without the other.
. Everything runs in containers on Kubernetes, in Google Cloud, described entirely by code and deployed by one pipeline.
. Every request and every order can be followed across services by one identifier, in logs, metrics and traces.
. The first deployment is a proof of concept that lasts two days, costs less than ten euros and destroys itself.

Each section below answers one question in a page and names the document where the detail is.

== How an order is placed

.Strategy question
****
What strategy lets four services complete one purchase without a transaction that spans them?
****

plantuml::diagrams/big-picture-order.puml[format=svg, align=center, pdfwidth=78%]

.The order of the steps is the design
[%unbreakable, cols="1,4,10", options="header"]
|===
| # | Step | Why there
| 1 | Reserve the stock | First, because a reservation is easy to undo. The brief's scenario of a paid order with no stock is avoided by construction: nobody is charged for what is not there.
| 2 | Charge the payment | The pivot. Before it, the saga can simply give up. After it, money is involved: the saga either finishes, refunds, or calls a person.
| 3 | Confirm the reservation | Can be repeated until it succeeds
| 4 | Confirm the order, e-mail the customer | Can be repeated until it succeeds
|===

.Three delays keep an order from waiting for ever
[%unbreakable, cols="4,3,8", options="header"]
|===
| Delay | Value | At its end
| Order deadline | 2 minutes | An order that is not paid is rejected, and whatever was started is undone. A payment found captured at that instant is carried through instead.
| Review delay | 5 minutes | An order that is paid and still not settled is shown "under review", and a person is alerted
| Reservation lifetime | 15 minutes | Stock held for an order that never completed goes back on sale
|===

The customer is answered at once with an order number; the page then asks for the outcome until the order is settled.

== How data stays consistent

.Strategy question
****
What strategy keeps money, stock and orders in agreement when every service has its own database?
****

There is no transaction across services, on purpose: it would tie the availability of each service to that of all the others ({ADR-PLT-011}).
Consistency is obtained by four mechanisms, each simple.

.Four mechanisms
[cols="3,7,5", options="header"]
|===
| Mechanism | What it does | Guarantees
| Outbox | A service writes its change and the messages announcing it in one local transaction; a relay publishes them afterwards | A message is never lost, and never sent for a change that did not happen
| Inbox and status | A service remembers which messages it handled, and answers a repeated request from the status of what it concerns | The bus may deliver twice; the effect happens once. Nobody is charged twice.
| Saga with compensations | The order service remembers where each order stands and what was done; each step has its undo | An order ends, one way or another: confirmed, rejected with everything undone, or handed to a person
| A grid of every event in every state | The outcome of each possible answer is written down for each state of the saga, late and duplicate answers included | No sequence of failures leads to an undefined situation
|===

.Five promises, tested as such
****
An order is confirmed only with a captured payment and a confirmed reservation. +
An order is rejected only when it holds no payment. +
A customer is charged at most once per order, and refunded at most once. +
Available stock is never negative. +
No order stays pending: after two minutes it is settled, and after five more a person knows.
****

== How errors are handled

.Strategy question
****
What strategy makes an error mean the same thing in every service, every log and every screen?
****

.One way to fail
[%unbreakable, cols="4,11", options="header"]
|===
| Subject | Rule
| One format | Every error answer has the same shape and carries a code of one catalog of twenty-nine codes. The storefront shows a message per code.
| Two kinds | A business refusal — out of stock, payment declined — is a normal result, with its own code. A technical failure is something else, and never looks like a refusal.
| The honest third case | When the platform does not know whether an action took effect, it says so, with a code of its own, instead of claiming that nothing changed
| Nothing internal leaks | An unexpected error returns a generic code and a reference that support can look up; never a stack trace
| Messages too | A message that cannot be handled is retried, then set aside in a dead-letter topic, with an alert; it never blocks the other orders
| A person, as the last step | What the platform cannot settle alone is shown under review to an administrator, who has three decisions and a runbook
|===

== What happens when something fails

.Strategy question
****
What strategy answers "what if this server dies?" for every piece of the platform?
****

.Failure by failure
[cols="4,6,5", options="header"]
|===
| Failure | What the platform does | What a customer sees
| A pod dies | Kubernetes starts another; the second replica serves meanwhile; messages the pod held are delivered again | Nothing, or one request to repeat
| A service is slow or overloaded | The caller gives up at its time limit; after repeated failures a circuit breaker stops calling for a while; a limit on concurrent calls keeps one slow neighbor from exhausting everything | A clear "try again", or a degraded answer: the cart shows the last known prices
| A service is down | Messages wait on the bus; orders in progress wait, then end at their deadlines | Orders are rejected uncharged, or shown under review; browsing continues if the catalog is up
| The payment provider does not answer | The payment is canceled, or found captured and carried through; if nobody can tell, the order goes under review | Never a charge without an order
| The message bus is unavailable | Services keep saving; messages wait in the outboxes and leave in order afterwards | Orders settle late, at their deadlines
| The database fails over | About a minute of clean refusals, then everything resumes | A minute of "try again"
| A zone is lost | Replicas in the other zones serve; the database fails over | The same minute
| The region is lost | Nothing automatic. The platform is rebuilt from code and from the backups, by a runbook. | An outage of hours, and the loss of the most recent data
|===

.Single points of failure, known and accepted
[%unbreakable, cols="4,11", options="header"]
|===
| Point | Decision
| One region, one cluster | Accepted by the product owner. A second region would double the cost for a store of this size.
| One database instance for all services and all environments | Accepted. Each service has its own database and account on it; in the full profile the instance has a standby in another zone.
| The proof of concept | One replica of everything, no standby: it demonstrates the design, not the availability. The availability requirements are specified for the full profile.
|===

.What Kubernetes does, and what it does not
[%unbreakable, cols="4,11", options="header"]
|===
| Kubernetes does | Restart a container that stops answering; keep the wanted number of replicas, spread over zones; send traffic only to pods that say they are ready; replace pods one at a time during an update, and stop if the new ones are not ready; add replicas when the processors are busy
| Kubernetes does not | Know that an order is stuck, that a message was lost, or that a customer was charged twice. That is the work of the saga, of the outbox, and of the alerts on business measures.
|===

== How we know what is going on

.Strategy question
****
What strategy lets one person follow one order through six services at three in the morning?
****

.Three signals, tied by two identifiers
[cols="3,6,6", options="header"]
|===
| Signal | Answers | How
| Logs | What happened, exactly? | Structured lines on standard output, collected by the platform. Every line carries the trace identifier and, for an order, the saga identifier.
| Metrics | How much, how fast, how often? | Counters and durations per service, and business measures: orders by outcome, orders under review, age of the oldest unpublished message
| Traces | Where did the time go, and through which services? | One trace per request, continued across the bus: the message carries the trace of the request that caused it
|===

The *trace identifier* follows a request; the *saga identifier* follows an order through all its requests and messages, from the checkout to the e-mail.
Searching the logs for a saga identifier gives the whole story of an order, across services, in order.

.From a number to an action
[%unbreakable, cols="4,11", options="header"]
|===
| Step | Meaning
| Objectives | A few numbers agreed with the business: 99.5 % of catalog reads answered; 99 % of valid checkouts accepted; 95 % of orders settled in less than 5 seconds
| Alerts | Fire when an objective is being missed fast, or when something specific needs a person: an order under review, a dead letter, a refund that failed
| Runbooks | Each alert names the procedure to follow
|===

== How it is deployed

.Strategy question
****
What strategy takes a change from a commit to production without a manual step that could be forgotten?
****

.One path
[%unbreakable, cols="1,4,10", options="header"]
|===
| # | Step | What it guarantees
| 1 | Pull request | Every gate passes before anyone reviews: tests, architecture rules, contracts, security scans. A person then reviews.
| 2 | Build, once | One image per service, signed. The same image goes everywhere.
| 3 | `test`, then `integration` | Deployed automatically, tested after each deployment
| 4 | Approval | A person approves production, with the evidence of the previous steps
| 5 | `prod` | One pod at a time; verified; returned to the previous version automatically if the verification fails
|===

Three environments live in one cluster, as three namespaces; the cloud resources and the Kubernetes objects are code, reviewed like the rest.
Nobody changes production by hand: engineers can read it, and only the pipeline can write.

== How it is secured

.Strategy question
****
What strategy protects customers' accounts and orders without building an identity system?
****

[%unbreakable, cols="4,11", options="header"]
|===
| Subject | Answer
| Sign-in | On the pages of the identity provider. No service of ours ever sees a password.
| The browser | Holds a session cookie that scripts cannot read. Tokens stay on the server side, in the web gateway.
| Every request | Carries the person's token to the service, which checks it and decides: denied unless explicitly allowed, and "not found" for what belongs to someone else
| Roles | Customer and administrator. An authorization matrix says, use case by use case, who may do what; a test is generated for each of its cells.
| Secrets | In a secret manager, never in code or configuration
| The network | One public entry; services are private and may only call what they are listed to call
| The software supply chain | Dependencies and images are scanned; only images built and signed by the pipeline can run
|===

== What it costs, and how it stops

.Strategy question
****
What strategy makes sure a demonstration of two days is billed for two days?
****

.Two profiles of the same design
[cols="4,5,6", options="header"]
|===
| | Proof of concept | Full profile
| Environments awake | One | Three
| Replicas | One of each | Two to six, autoscaled
| Database | One small instance, no standby | With a standby in another zone
| Cost | About 0.18 USD per hour: about 8.50 USD for 48 hours | From about 500 USD per month with production awake
| If forgotten | About 130 USD in a month | –
|===

A budget only warns, and its figures arrive hours late.
The proof of concept therefore carries its own end: a deadline given when it is created, after which the pipeline destroys everything.
The two profiles differ by values only; going from one to the other changes no code.

== How it is built, and proven

.Strategy question
****
What strategy keeps the business rules testable in a millisecond, and everything else replaceable?
****

.Inside a service
[%unbreakable, cols="3,12", options="header"]
|===
| Ring | Content
| Domain | The business rules, in plain Java, depending on nothing. Covered at 100 % by unit tests that run in milliseconds.
| Application | The use cases: one per thing an actor wants. They say what is needed from outside through ports.
| Adapters | REST, messaging, persistence, calls to others. Each can be replaced: the catalog and the cart run on PostgreSQL, on a document database or on files, behind the same ports.
|===

Twenty-six architecture rules, checked at every build, keep the rings apart.

.The proof
[%unbreakable, cols="4,11", options="header"]
|===
| Level | Proves
| Unit | Every business rule and every cell of the saga grids
| Acceptance | The ninety-six scenarios agreed with the business, written before the code
| Component | Each service keeps its contracts, with its real adapters
| Integration | The pieces work with the real technology; the nineteen saga scenarios, failures provoked
| End to end | The six critical paths, in a browser, on a deployed environment
|===

Every use case, rule, scenario and test has an identifier, and the build of this specification refuses a rule without a test, or a test that nothing builds.

The platform is built by AI agents, in forty-three increments, each checked by gates, by independent expert agents and by a person; the agents work in a harness that leaves them no right on the cloud.
